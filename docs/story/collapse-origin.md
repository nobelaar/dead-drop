# Origin of the crisis — DEAD DROP: BLACKOUT

**Status:** Provisional narrative **proposal**. Pending iteration and approval.  
**Document type:** Design hypothesis (not final universe fact)  
**Priority:** Directional only until explicitly promoted to canon

## Purpose

This document proposes an initial direction for explaining the origin of the global crisis in DEAD DROP: BLACKOUT, the actors involved, and the leads that might draw **Nicolás “Nico” Ferreyra** into a wider investigation.

**It is not definitive canon.** Ideas, organizations, names, and explanations here are narrative design hypotheses that must be reviewed before they become established universe facts.

This document **complements** [overview.md](overview.md). It does not replace it and must not duplicate that file’s general content.

**Continuity note (author instruction):** Keep this proposal separate from facts already established in `overview.md`. Do **not** automatically rewrite character bios, definitive timeline, missions, or other existing docs to force-fit this hypothesis. When a pending question is decided, update the relevant docs modularly and keep them consistent.

Goal: a convincing explanation of the collapse and its consequences — not closing the mystery too early, and not turning every institution and character into pieces of a single conspiracy.

## 1. Central hypothesis

The global crisis arises from a mix of technical vulnerabilities, infrastructure interdependencies, human decisions, and deliberate actions by actors with their own interests.

In the years before the game’s events, various operators of electrical infrastructure, telecommunications, and digital services adopted increasingly interdependent coordination systems. There is no single world network that controls all of those services, but certain providers, components, and information-exchange mechanisms are shared across multiple organizations.

That concentration of dependencies creates fragility. A localized failure can propagate when several systems rely on incorrect information, lose coordination channels, or react incompatibly to an emergency.

The crisis does **not** require someone who can shut off the planet with one command. Its scale comes from the interaction of complex systems, protection mechanisms, operational decisions, and failures that reinforce each other.

Narrative hypothesis: some actors knew some of these vulnerabilities; others helped trigger or worsen the crisis for motives still to be defined.

**Not decided:** whether the global collapse was the goal of a deliberate operation, an unexpected consequence of a more limited action, or a systemic catastrophe later exploited by various groups.

## 2. Protocol Threshold (Protocolo Umbral)

**Protocol Threshold** (*Protocolo Umbral*) is the provisional name of a fictional operational-continuity program.

**Official purpose:** coordinate public and private response to large-scale emergencies — information exchange, isolation of compromised systems, prioritization of essential services.

The program does **not** directly control all world infrastructure. Its relevance comes from relationships with certain operators, information it can access, and influence over recovery decisions.

Central tension: the same tools that keep essential services running in an emergency can concentrate power, restrict information, or decide which regions, institutions, and communities get resources first.

**Still pending:**

- Who created and funded the program
- Which organizations participate, and under what terms
- Real technical capabilities
- What information was available before the crisis
- Whether prioritization reflected legitimate emergency criteria, economic interests, or political decisions
- Whether the program was deliberately used to worsen the collapse, or its limits were merely exposed during the crisis

Must **not** be presented automatically as an evil organization or as the confirmed cause of the blackout.

## 3. Involved actors

Three **categories** of actors are proposed. These are narrative functions — not necessarily three unique organizations or permanent alliances.

### 3.1. The Consortium

Provisional name for a fictional coalition of tech companies, infrastructure providers, and advisors with institutional influence.

Some members may have known important vulnerabilities and prioritized commercial continuity, reputation, or strategic interests over transparency.

Not everyone need share information or blame. Some may have hidden risks; some may have been negligent; some may not grasp how bad it was.

That certain members **benefit** from the crisis does **not** prove they caused it.

### 3.2. The group that started the operation

An actor still without defined name or identity gained access to certain coordination components shared by several operators.

Motivation and original goal remain open. Might have sought strategic advantage, a limited interruption, compromised institutional capacity, or another aim that did not require destroying world infrastructure.

The operation helps trigger or worsen the crisis; it is **not** decided whether global consequences were intentional, foreseen, or unexpected.

Must not have omnipotent capabilities or arbitrary access to every system.

### 3.3. Crisis administrators

Category covering officials, emergency leads, operational command, and private providers tasked with containing damage and restoring services.

Motives vary. Some try to prevent further casualties; some restrict information for operational or political reasons; some use the emergency to expand authority or favor certain groups.

Decisions may be necessary, abusive, or both, depending on context and outcomes.

Must not function as a uniformly good or evil faction.

## 4. Hypothetical development of the crisis

Working sequence — **not** a definitive chronology.

1. **Prior vulnerabilities.** Certain shared components and coordination procedures accumulate weaknesses without an adequate response. Some organizations know part of the risk; no sufficient fix is reached.
2. **Initial access.** A group gains access to part of the shared digital infrastructure. Exact nature of access and how long it stays hidden: TBD.
3. **Trigger.** A deliberate operation, combined with existing failures, causes incidents affecting various operators. Not all systems react the same way.
4. **Propagation.** Protection measures, preventive disconnects, and loss of coordination help interruptions spread unevenly across regions and services.
5. **Information crisis.** Operators, institutions, and the public lose a shared, reliable picture of events. Rumors, contradictory explanations, decisions on incomplete information.
6. **Fight over recovery.** Actors who still have resources, communications, or operational capacity start shaping what is restored and who gets aid.
7. **Opportunism and cover-up.** Some groups use the situation for power or to avoid prior liability. Still TBD which of those moves were planned and which emerged after collapse.

The crisis should feel global without implying every country loses all power and communications at once. Consequences vary by local infrastructure, backup sources, geography, and each operator’s decisions.

## 5. Nico’s investigation

Nico does **not** start the campaign knowing Threshold or holding conspiracy proof. Investigation grows from failures he sees on his workday.

Discovery must be progressive and rest on concrete evidence.

### Stage 1 — Technical anomalies

Errors, contradictory logs, operations whose results disagree across systems.

At first several explanations are plausible: sync failures, data corruption, infrastructure problems, or intrusion.

### Stage 2 — Shared patterns

Comparing sources, Nico sees similarities among incidents that looked independent.

Patterns justify digging further; they do **not** automatically name a culprit.

### Stage 3 — Earlier warnings

Signs that certain vulnerabilities were identified before the crisis.

Some reports may have been filed away, minimized, or restricted. Evidence should allow distinguishing negligence, deliberate cover-up, and decisions under uncertainty.

### Stage 4 — References to Threshold

Nico finds references to the program and learns some actors had coordination mechanisms, privileged information, or recovery procedures not public.

Investigation shows uneven resources across groups — still does **not** prove Threshold caused the collapse.

### Stage 5 — Contradictions

Evidence does not fully match the official story, but does not confirm every suspicion either.

Nico must decide what to share, how to verify, whom to trust, and what risks to take for more proof.

Leads must not confirm every player theory. Separate verifiable facts, reasonable inference, interested testimony, and speculation.

## 6. The recovery conflict

A central story question: **who decides what is restored first** when resources are limited.

Protocol Threshold might hold prioritization mechanisms meant to keep essential services running. Those same mechanisms might favor certain facilities, regions, institutions, or economic interests.

Nico might find docs framing these choices as technical emergency measures. Later evidence might suggest some priorities served interests other than public safety.

Interpretation stays open until enough proof exists.

Unequal recovery priority alone does **not** prove conspiracy: it may be real limits, bad assessment, debatable criteria, or deliberately unjust choice.

Narrative goal: turn an apparently administrative issue into a concrete human conflict when consequences hit Nico and his friends.

---

## 7. Relation to characters

Crisis effects should fit each character’s skills, resources, and circumstances.

Nico’s technical knowledge lets him investigate systems and understand some evidence — not alone solve electrical, logistics, medical, or political problems.

Santiago, Tomás, Bruno, Vale, and Lucía must not become simple clue dispensers. Their lives, goals, and decisions keep autonomy.

Investigation into the collapse’s origin may cross their needs and Nico’s choices; not everyone need connect directly to Threshold or hold secret knowledge.

Global conflict raises pressure on relationships without replacing the campaign’s human core.

---

## 8. Coherence principles

- No single button instantly kills all electrical systems on the planet.
- Infrastructure has real dependencies, physical limits, and protection mechanisms.
- Technology enables certain actions but still needs access, tools, knowledge, and time.
- Involved groups have limited resources and capabilities.
- Benefiting from the crisis ≠ having caused it.
- Important narrative conclusions need evidence that can support them.
- Information may be incomplete or manipulated; mystery must not rely on arbitrary contradictions.
- Institutional decisions can have grave consequences without every one being a conspiracy.
- Global scale shows in consequences without erasing regional differences.
- No organization knows or controls everything that happens.

4. Which actor knows the fullest explanation of the crisis, and what proof backs that knowledge?
5. What really happened in the first hours, and what official explanations were circulated?
